# The Secret Agent: who answers when AI starts acting inside organizations?

When we talk about artificial intelligence in companies, the conversation usually starts with the capability of the technology.

What can it write? Which tasks can it automate? How much time can it save? Which decisions can it support?

With the evolution of AI agents, however, we need to ask a more important question:

**What happens when artificial intelligence stops only answering and starts acting?**

An assistant receives a request and returns an answer. An agent can receive an objective, plan steps, look up information, use tools, make intermediate decisions, and execute actions in the environment.

That difference completely changes the discussion.

From that moment on, we are not dealing only with the adoption of a tool. We are talking about the delegation of authority inside organizations.

## The agent can act, but it cannot take on the responsibility

We can delegate the execution of a task. We can also allow an agent to choose paths within previously defined limits.

What we cannot delegate is responsibility for the outcome.

An agent does not take part in a crisis meeting to explain its decisions. It does not answer a harmed customer, does not take on a sanction, does not repair a relationship of trust, and cannot be held professionally accountable for the consequences of its actions.

That is why one idea needs to remain at the center of this discussion:

**Autonomy can be delegated. Accountability cannot.**

Every agent used in an organizational context needs to have, at a minimum, a process or product owner, a technical owner, and clear rules for supervision and escalation.

When everyone participates but no one answers, the technology only makes more evident a problem that already existed in the design of the organization.

## The risk is not only in the model

Many companies still assess the risks of AI thinking mainly about the quality of the model being used.

But, in agentic systems, the risk depends much more on what the agent can do.

Can it only look up a piece of information, or can it also change it? Can it draft a message or send it directly to thousands of customers? Can it suggest a fix or publish a new version to production? Can it analyze a transaction or move money? Can it recommend removing an access or actually delete an account?

The greater the capacity to interfere with the world, the greater the combination of control, traceability, supervision, and the possibility of reversal needs to be.

We should not classify risk by the model alone. We need to consider everything the agent can see, decide, change, buy, publish, send, or delete.

## Human supervision cannot be only a formality

It is common to hear that a given process remains safe because there is a person responsible for the final approval.

But is that supervision really effective?

If a person needs to review hundreds of decisions per hour, without enough context and pressed for time, there is probably no human supervision. There is only a mechanical confirmation used to transfer responsibility.

Supervising requires four elements:

- Time to analyze
- Context to understand
- Competence to question
- Authority to interrupt

Without these elements, the human being becomes only a bureaucratic step inside an essentially automated decision.

## New actors require more explicit responsibilities

The adoption of agents does not necessarily mean creating a new role for each responsibility. It means ensuring that no responsibility is left orphaned.

The executive sponsor needs to define the risk appetite and explain why it is worth taking that risk.

The product or process owner must answer for the outcome and for the impact on the user.

Technical leadership needs to guarantee architecture, integration, observability, quality, and continuity.

The security, privacy, data, and legal areas need to take part in defining the limits, and not only analyze the problem after something has gone wrong.

The people manager also has an important role. AI changes activities, evaluation criteria, skills, and working relationships. Adoption cannot be treated exclusively as a technical initiative.

And the professional responsible for supervision needs to have real conditions to intervene.

The question should not be only “is there a human in the process?”, but:

**Does this human have the context, time, and authority to take control?**

## The software development example

Let us think about an agent responsible for reviewing code.

It can analyze pull requests, identify vulnerabilities, suggest fixes, and check patterns. That can certainly increase a team's capacity.

But what happens when the same agent writes the code, creates the tests, and also performs the review?

Is there still an independent evaluation, or only a validation done by the same system that produced the solution?

The same dilemma appears in incident management.

An agent can correlate logs, formulate hypotheses, run diagnostics, and apply reversible actions set out in a runbook.

But should it carry out on its own a destructive change, or one with a large blast radius?

At what point does speed stop being an advantage and start amplifying the error?

These questions show that governance does not need to mean slowness. Good controls are precisely what makes it possible to grant autonomy safely.

## The guardrails that make autonomy possible

An agent operating in an organization should have its own identity, minimal permissions, and a clear audit trail.

We also need to define in advance:

- Which data it can access
- Which tools it can use
- Which actions are allowed
- Which actions are forbidden
- When it needs to request approval
- What the limit of volume, frequency, or value is
- How its decisions will be monitored
- How to interrupt its operation
- How to reverse an incorrect action
- Who should be called in case of an incident

These controls do not completely eliminate errors. They reduce the blast radius, improve the capacity for detection, and allow a faster response.

A good organization is not the one that stops its agents from making mistakes, whether they are human or artificial. It is the one that limits the consequences, identifies problems quickly, learns from incidents, and does not hide responsibility inside technical complexity.

## The impact on leadership

The use of agents does not diminish the importance of leadership. It changes its focus.

Leading stops meaning only assigning tasks and tracking their execution. It comes to involve defining objectives, limits, success criteria, and control mechanisms.

Instead of tracking only deliveries, we need to observe decisions and exceptions.

Instead of reviewing only the final result, we need to evaluate the entire process built between people and artificial intelligence.

Instead of measuring productivity exclusively, we need to balance speed, quality, security, and trust.

The role of leadership also becomes that of designing work systems in which people and agents can collaborate without responsibility becoming invisible.

## Human judgment remains indispensable

Artificial intelligence can drastically reduce the cost of execution. But that makes judgment even more valuable.

The professional does not lose relevance by using AI. They lose relevance when they transfer to it the responsibility of thinking, questioning, and deciding.

We need to avoid both the complete rejection of the technology and adoption without criteria. Mature organizations create safe environments for experimentation, increase autonomy as evidence appears, and keep clear paths for interruption and correction.

Before we ask what an agent is able to do, we need to answer:

**What may it do?**

**What must it not do?**

**Who can interrupt it?**

**And who answers when that boundary is crossed?**

Perhaps that is the true “secret agent” inside organizations: not the artificial intelligence operating behind the scenes, but the responsibility that disappears when no one knows exactly on whose behalf it is acting.
