# Developers, analysts, and DBAs: slaves to technology in the age of AI?

*A reflection on continuous learning, career choices, and what changes when artificial intelligence enters daily work.*

In 2010, I wrote a piece after a conversation with a coworker. We were discussing the need to study continuously in order to keep up with technology. For him, the obligation to always learn felt like a prison. The way out would be to move into management.

So many years later, the question has only become more interesting. Today, besides new languages, architectures, and ways of working, artificial intelligence takes part in writing code, analyzing data, documentation, and even review steps. Has that made us even more dependent on technology? Or has it changed the way we need to learn?

## Learning is part of the work — including in management

I still believe that professionals in any field need to set aside room to grow. That does not mean taking a course every week, mastering every tool that ships, or turning free time into an extension of the workday.

It means keeping curiosity and noticing when the knowledge that supports our decisions needs an update. We can learn from a book, from a conversation with someone on the team, by investigating an incident, or by trying an idea on a project. There are many ways to grow; none of them requires chasing every novelty.

Moving into management does not remove that need either. People who lead teams or projects have to follow changes in the work, understand the team's challenges, and make decisions in contexts that also evolve. Management can be a very good career choice, as long as it comes from an interest in its own challenges, not from the expectation that there it will be possible to stop learning.

## AI changes the speed, but it does not take the responsibility

AI tools already help explore solutions, draft text, explain pieces of code, write tests, and organize information. There are real gains, but the quality of the result depends on the context in which the tool is used and on the ability to judge what it produced. Recent [DORA research on AI-assisted software development](https://cloud.google.com/resources/content/2025-dora-ai-assisted-software-development-report) shows both a sense of productivity and lingering doubts about trust in generated code.

If an AI proposes a change to an API, someone still has to understand the requirement, check the effects on users, assess security and maintenance, test the behavior, and decide whether that change should reach production. The same applies to a data analysis, an architecture recommendation, or a project plan. The tool can collaborate; responsibility for the decision stays with people and with the organization.

That is why learning to use AI is not just about writing good prompts. It includes knowing how to give context, recognize limits, check sources, protect data, and spot when a convincing answer is wrong. It also includes knowing when to solve something without it.

## The risk is confusing staying current with an endless race

In 2010, I said we should not simply let the years go by. I still think that. Today I would add a caution: keeping up with the profession cannot mean living in a permanent state of urgency.

We do not need to know every tool to stay relevant. We need solid fundamentals and criteria for choosing the next thing to learn. For a developer, that may mean understanding the product, the architecture, or software quality better before adopting yet another coding assistant. For someone who leads, it may mean making room for experimentation, setting criteria for how AI is used, and helping the team share what works — and what does not.

The useful question may be: **what do I need to learn now in order to do my work better and make more conscious decisions?** The answer changes with the moment in a career, the team, and the problems we want to solve.

## So, are we slaves to technology?

We would be if we accepted every novelty as an order, with no room to choose, question, or set limits. Continuous learning, on the other hand, can be a way to keep that choice in our hands.

AI makes this discussion more urgent. It expands what we can try, but it also demands more discernment about what we ship. The professional who grows is not necessarily the one who uses more tools or studies for more hours. It is the one who can connect knowledge, context, and responsibility in order to create real value.

**And you: has AI given your work more autonomy, or increased the feeling that it is impossible to keep up with everything?**

## Original article — September 19, 2010

### Developers, analysts, DBAs: slaves to technology?

Some time ago, I had a discussion with a coworker about the growth that we, technology professionals, should pursue so the market does not swallow us. My view is clear: I believe every professional, regardless of the field they work in, should devote a share of their time to improvement and professional growth. That can happen directly, by acquiring knowledge, or indirectly, by learning from other people. What matters is not simply letting the years go by.

During the discussion, I got the impression that my coworker believed that, because he always had to "learn new things" and "never stop studying," he was trapped by technology, as its slave. For him, the solution would be to turn to management.

I do not know in detail the routine of a project or people manager. But, with a simple bit of research, I saw that the market changes for those professionals too. New methodologies and ways of carrying out their work appear and get adopted. Besides that, you need to know what you are managing — and, in the case of technology, that object is constantly evolving. Updating knowledge through courses or reading is therefore also necessary in management. Even the experience gained at work, day after day, can count as a form of improvement.

We can go further. A historian, for example, works in a profession that studies the past. They might spend years without taking a new course or starting a project, but could they practice their profession well without doing new research in their specialty? I do not think so.

Market growth and the arrival of new technologies are inevitable. Tools improve; trends appear and disappear. In some way, the professional needs to follow those changes. Periodic reading, or taking part in a workshop, can add a great deal. You only have to want to.
